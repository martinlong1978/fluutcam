# Drop Speed, Round 2 Plan

> **For agentic workers:** REQUIRED SUB-SKILL: Use superpowers:subagent-driven-development (recommended) or superpowers:executing-plans to implement this plan task-by-task. Steps use checkbox (`- [x]`) syntax for tracking.

**Goal:** First measure whole 3-D finishing toolpaths on curved parts (how much of their time is the drop query, how they scale with threads, what adaptive sampling could save), then a second round on the drop query on free-form models ([D07](../design/D07-cutter-queries.md) §8, [CH-25](../design/challenges.md)): 25-66 µs median after the [B-spline speed plan](2026-11-11-bspline-speed.md) against a 20 µs target. Adaptive sampling is a separate later task; here it is only estimated.

**Architecture:** Measured first; instrumentation for the drop round (outcome and iteration counters of the Newton solvers, timers around the re-solves) was not kept. The finishing benchmark is kept behind a feature.

*Finishing benchmark (kept).* Feature `bench-stats` of `fluut-cam` counts and times every `SurfaceQuery` drop (summed over threads) and can record the drop points; it is off in every normal build. `examples/finishing_bench.rs` (needs the feature) runs whole toolpaths through `calculate` on one thread and on all (`rayon` pools) and estimates adaptive sampling on `parallel` passes (see Part 1).

*Flat-bottom Newton gives up at its second clamped step (kept, heuristic, verified).* `newton_flat` looks for a stationary point of `z − ε·d²` under the flat bottom (flat and bull tools) over the face's parameter box, clamping every step to the box. Traced: its non-cycling 40-iteration failures were orbits of period 3 or so through a clamp on the box edge (the stationary point of the surface lies beyond the face), closing up geometrically without ever repeating a point's bits, so the cycle exit of the last round never fired. Counted on the drop_bench rasters (3 x 6 000 drops per tool, 3 mm sculpted model, bull): of 315 000 runs, 75 % failed by cycle or 40 iterations, using 85 % of the iterations; 22 % "converged" on the box (a clamped step that did not move), 3 % in the interior. Experiments, each compared drop by drop with the base on all 54 000 raster drops: refusing the converged-on-the-box points changed no drop at all (they are not stationary points: the face boundary is the edges' job); giving up at the first clamped step changed 21 drops, none by more than 1e-7 mm; at the second, 2 drops, none by more than 1e-7 mm. The second was kept for margin (most of the speed of the first). It is not a proof: an iteration that is clamped twice and would come back to an interior stationary point is lost; such points were always found from another start or patch in every check (see After). Skipping the flat Newton altogether moved 1.4 to 4.6 % of the flat and bull drops lower, by up to 0.4 mm, and lost some contacts entirely: it is needed.

*Proven edge sub-boxes (kept, proven).* `BSplineCurve3::segment_bounds` bounds each of the 32 segments of a B-spline edge by the boxes of the Bézier points of the curve restricted to its part of each span it crosses (de Casteljau blossoms of the cached Bézier spans; convex hull property, weights positive; grown by 1e-13 relative for rounding). They replace the sampled boxes (end points grown by the doubled sampled second derivative). Tighter too: 4-26 % fewer edge solves per drop. A range outside the domain, or a degree above 7, keeps the sampled boxes.

*Tighter strip bound for re-solves (tried, dropped).* Before re-solving a suspect patch, the boundary-strip cells whose bound beats the best contact were split into 2 x 2 or 4 x 4 cells bounded by their Bézier points (proven, the same region: the rule stays the same, only the bound is tighter), with an early exit at the first cell that still beats the best. It skipped 30 % (2 x 2) to 50 % (4 x 4) of the re-solves, changed no drop by more than 1e-7 mm, but was no faster: a re-solve costs about 10 µs and the subdivision of the 5-15 cells concerned about as much (1-2 µs a cell at 4 x 4), even made allocation-free. Running the re-solve's starts one by one and skipping starts whose cell no longer beats a contact found meanwhile gave nothing either: re-solves almost never raise the best contact, so the bound never moves.

**Findings.**
- On free-form parts 91-99 % of a finishing toolpath's time is in drops; linking, classification, filtering and arc fitting are 1-9 %. On the analytic parts drops are 72-99 % (the drop is 1-15 µs there, so the rest shows). Making the drop faster is still the lever; nothing else is worth measuring for speed now.
- **`parallel`, `spiral` and `scallop` run on one thread** (scaling 1.0: their passes are sampled in a plain loop). Waterline `contour` scales 5.5-7.4 times on 12 threads on smooth parts (levels in parallel), 2.6-2.9 on the rough tiles (a few levels hold most of the work); `steep_and_shallow` 1.5-4.2 (its shallow part is a one-thread `parallel`/`scallop`). The drop keeps no state, so the passes of `parallel` (and the rings of `spiral`, the offsets of `scallop` within a ring) could run in parallel and keep their order; for `parallel` this would give about the waterline's 6 times on 12 threads. Not part of this plan: a separate task.
- Drops per second per thread: 11 500-33 000 on free-form models (flat tools fastest, bull slowest), 65 000-830 000 on analytic ones.
- The flat and bull tools on `rough3d` (an analytic part with cylinders and tori) cost 9-10 µs per drop against 1.4-1.8 µs for the ball: the numeric solve (no closed form for a flat or bull tool on a cylinder or torus).

**Adaptive sampling, estimate.** The `parallel` sampler (`follow::Sampler`) is already adaptive in one way: it bisects a chord while its midpoint is more than `tol/4` off it or it is longer than `R/2`, and the midpoint drop of an accepted chord is thrown away. So it takes 2.2-3.3 drops per output CL point (sculpted 3 mm ball: 59 700 drops, 25 200 CL points). After the fact, over all the drops each pass line received (sorted along the line): a drop within the chord tolerance of the chord between its two neighbours is 93-100 % of them, which only says the samples are dense; the useful figure is a greedy chord merge over all of them (keep a point only where the chord from the last kept point would leave one of the points between by more than the tolerance), which an ideal sampler would still need:

| Model | Tool | Drops on pass lines | Left out at tol/4 (0.0025 mm) | at tol (0.01 mm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sculpted 3 mm | flat / ball / bull | 55-59 k | 65 / 63 / 64 % | 83 % |
| sculpted 12 mm | flat / ball / bull | 53-56 k | 64 / 62 / 63 % | 82-83 % |
| rough tiles | flat / ball / bull | 135-184 k | 81 / 67 / 69 % | 83-88 % |
| dome | all | 37-38 k | 76-77 % | 88-89 % |
| nist_ctc_02 | flat / ball | 2.5 / 3.0 M | 82 / 75 % | 91 / 88 % |

So an ideal sampler at the same chord tolerance (`tol/4`) would need about a third of the drops on smooth free-form parts (a quarter or less on the rough tiles and on analytic parts with large flat areas). A real one needs some drops to check its steps; predicting the step from the last three CL points (the second difference gives the local curvature, so the step that keeps the chord within `tol/4`) and checking it with the next drop instead of a midpoint that is thrown away should get to about 1.2-1.5 drops per needed point, that is **40-55 % fewer drops (1.7 to 2.2 times faster `parallel` finishing)** on smooth free-form parts. It must keep the gouge guarantee of the bisection (a chord's midpoint drop is a check that the chord does not cut into the part between samples): the check drop must be one the next chord also uses, not dropped. **Recommendation:** worth a separate task after making `parallel`, `spiral` and `scallop` multi-threaded (cheaper, larger, no change to results); do it for the line and loop drives in `follow.rs` together, with the gouge check against today's sampler on the finishing tests and the bench models.
